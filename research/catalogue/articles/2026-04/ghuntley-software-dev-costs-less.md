## Key Takeaways

1. **The $10.42/Hour Thesis** — AI models have commoditized software development skills to the point where the effective cost of creating software is below minimum wage. This is not a prediction — it's already observable in how private equity firms are shorting traditional software companies (the "Ralph" discovery leading to Atlassian shorts).

2. **K-Shaped Economic Divide** — Two categories of companies are emerging: (1) model-first companies operating as lean "apex predators" on razor-thin margins with small teams, and (2) traditional companies requiring massive transformation programs and vulnerable to displacement. There is no middle ground.

3. **Compressed Competition Timelines** — When anyone can replicate an entire SaaS product in weeks, the competitive moat of features and code evaporates. Competitors can clone your product faster than you can ship the next version. This creates a continuous displacement loop.

4. **Identity Erasure and Workforce Consequences** — Professional identities built over decades (backend dev, frontend dev, team lead, manager) lose market value overnight. One anonymous founder reported reducing staff by two-thirds while achieving 30x output. Workers face a hard fork: upskill into AI-native workflows or become obsolete.

5. **New Moat Taxonomy** — Per-seat pricing, human-centric feature design, and technology switching costs are no longer moats. What remains: distribution networks, brand awareness, utility-based pricing models, and model-first operational principles. This reframes the entire SaaS value proposition.

## Summary

Geoffrey Huntley argues that AI has permanently altered the unit economics of software development. The catalyst: tools like Cursor and Claude Code have made it possible for non-developers to create production software, while experienced developers can operate at orders of magnitude higher output. He cites a private equity firm that profited substantially by shorting Atlassian after discovering "Ralph" (an AI tool that undermined traditional dev tool business models).

The central framework is a "K-shaped divide" — model-first companies (lean, AI-native, operating with 5-person teams doing what previously required 50) will outcompete traditional organizations that still staff up with layers of engineers, leads, and managers. Huntley cites a Cursor meetup where attendees were primarily non-developers building real applications, as evidence that specialized programming knowledge has been democratized.

The article is deliberately provocative about workforce consequences. Professional identities built over decades are being erased. The author questions the value of senior engineers, team leads, managers, and directors when AI handles most routine engineering work. An anonymous founder reportedly cut two-thirds of staff while achieving 30x output gains.

On business moats, Huntley argues that per-seat pricing (Atlassian, Jira) is dead because AI agents don't need seats. Features designed for human workflows are trivially replicable. Technology switching costs vanish when AI can automate migrations (he cites a Cloudflare D1 to PlanetScale migration). The surviving moats are distribution, brand, and utility-based pricing — which is why cloud infrastructure companies (AWS, Cloudflare) are better positioned than SaaS tool vendors.

The article concludes with an unequivocal stance: "Things will change, and there's no going back. The unit economics of business have forever changed."
